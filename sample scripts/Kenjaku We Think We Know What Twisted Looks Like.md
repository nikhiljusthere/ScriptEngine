**Kenjaku We Think We Know What Twisted Looks Like**

We think we know what twisted looks like. Twisted is the shape pain leaves when it sets. A villain bent around the wound until cruelty is the only posture left to him. Something was done to him and everything he becomes is an echo of that something. Twisted in a way is a cost and because it is a cost, we can almost forgive it. However, there is a worse kind of twisted and it's one where the story never lets you find the wound. You will look, you will wait, you'll question. It wants you to look. A man this monstrous must have a first page, a prologue, a starting point, a hand that shaped him, an idea that nurtured him. Yet the manga just refuses to hand it over. Kenjaku is over a thousand years old with no first face, with no full name, no exact birth date, no historical renown, no reputation. No one remembers making him. Maybe a wound is buried back there. And maybe not. We're never shown it. Whatever the story gave its other monsters, a reason, a grief, a breaking point, a cursed nature, it withholds from this one. He arrives already finished, already curious, with no before, with no starting point. What is left is a man who did unspeakable things and felt nothing while he did them. Nothing but a mild, pleasant curiosity, the kind you'd feel watching a kettle waiting for it to boil. He is not twisted because something twisted him. As far as the story ever shows, he simply grew this way and he was having a wonderful time. There is a moment near the end of the story when Kenjaku is battling Takaba in a life or death exchange, standing on a stage telling jokes, an actual stage, an actual comedy routine. Kenjaku has one half of a two-man act. Takaba's cursed technique bends the world into whatever he finds funny. And instead of trying to beat it, Kenjaku steps inside and plays along. They walk out under the lights and start their bits. He riffs on Momotaro, improvises a tearful backstory to derail his partner, argues over punchlines, and he is loving it. Kenjaku says it out loud himself at the end. This is the most fun he's had in centuries. And this stupid routine has made all the trouble of the Culling Game worth it. The man who spent a thousand years engineering the end of the world, the ascension of man, the evolution of cursed-infused humanity, having fun through it all. And the best time he ever had was a bit on a stage.

The thing I find most interesting here is the Momotaro bit. It is something that has been stuck in my mind ever since this fight ended and I struggled to find meaning in until now because it is an absurd comedy act. But perhaps there is something hidden there as well. The fight or the showdown is one of the most surreal sequences, using Japan's most famous children's tale to stage a literal life or death battle. To understand the brilliance of this moment, one must first understand Momotaro, the Peach Boy. In traditional Japanese folklore, Momotaro is a miraculous hero born from a giant peach who journeys to the demon island Onigashima to slay terrorizing ogres. Along the way, he uses his mother's homemade kibidango, millet dumplings, to recruit three loyal animal companions: a dog, a monkey, and a pheasant, who all help him achieve a righteous, swift victory. It is a foundational myth about absolute good triumphing over absolute evil through unity and moral clarity. When Kenjaku and Takaba perform a manzai, a traditional stand-up routine based on this fable, they deliberately strip Momotaro of its moral weight by overanalyzing the logistics of the myth itself, such as Kenjaku refusing to eat the kibidango because someone else touched them with their bare hands, or introducing a Western breed of dog into an ancient Japanese tale. The duo deconstructs a sacred cultural narrative into pure absurdity. On a deeper narrative level, this routine serves several hidden thematic purposes. First is subverting the hero's journey. The Momotaro tale mirrors the typical shonen manga structure where a righteous hero gathers allies to defeat a great evil. They subvert it by turning this exact story into a joke. Gege Akutami signals that the traditional rules of hero versus villain battles do not apply here. It also hints at Kenjaku's ancient connection. Kenjaku is a millennium-old sorcerer who has lived through the very eras these folktales were born in. To him, they are not folktales. His cynical, overly practical critique of Momotaro highlights his own detachment from human sentimentality. He doesn't see the myth as an inspiring tale of good. He sees it as a flawed narrative, echoing his overall philosophy regarding humanity and the Culling Game, with an added twist to Momotaro being the origin of the hero's story and Kenjaku questioning his birth and parenthood. Kenjaku, who is responsible for the birth and parenthood of this tale's hero. The choice of Momotaro is a massive structural foreshadowing of Kenjaku's own demise as well because in the fable, Momotaro and his animal spirits launch a surprise attack on the demon king's fortress. In this comedic adaptation, Kenjaku gets so completely absorbed in playing the demon king role in their sketch that he misses the actual literal ambush. Ultimately, the comedy routine in itself acts as a psychological trap. Takaba's technique forces a reality where Kenjaku must satisfy his ancient, deep-seated desire for entertainment. And by successfully engaging Kenjaku in a deconstruction of Japan's ultimate victory story, Takaba lowers the villain's guard. The curtain falls on their Momotaro bit. And before Kenjaku can step out of the fiction, Okkotsu Yuta arrives like a true folklore hero to decapitate him, proving that Kenjaku could outsmart jujutsu sorcery, but he couldn't survive being the punchline.

So, let's ask the obvious question. What was done to him? Every villain in this series is an answer to something. Geto broke under the weight of what he could see and no one else could. Mahito was born from the plain fact that people hate each other. Even Sukuna is the echo of an age that starved and a womb that couldn't feed him. Pull the thread off any of them and you find the origin point. Pull Kenjaku's thread and he comes back with nothing on the end of it other than a smiling brain. We don't know his first name, his first face, his first cause, his first victim, whether he was ever wrong, whether he had a first, whether he ever grieved, ever wanted something the world took from him. The story doesn't say. And that silence is what I find most fascinating about him. And it is the first true thing about him. And it isn't only that we can't find the wound. It's that he doesn't move like a man carrying a wound. When Geto's twin daughters corner him, the girls who loved the man whose body he wore, they told him he would regret this. He comments after they leave, when no one is around to hear him, that he forgot what regret even is in the heart of Shibuya after accomplishing one of his most meticulous and important plans. He patiently sits cross-legged waiting for the Prison Realm to fully process Gojo so he can leave while chaos and death explode all around him in the incident he engineered. Yet he is sat alone in the station, musing on the fact that he forgot what regret even is. Regret is what's left when you wanted things to go differently. And nothing has ever gone differently to Kenjaku because he never needed it to. The most terrifying antagonist in a twisted dark story is just a man doing what he finds interesting. Driven only by pure curiosity. No hatred, no revenge, no grudge nourished across a thousand years. Kenjaku spends the lead-up playing games, blowing bubbles in a park, Monopoly, card games, board games, football. It isn't a turn of phrase. He sits down with the curses, plays games, and offers advice. He is good company. Yet the entire time he views them as beneath him, ready to spend them like loose change. The moment they lose their utility, none of it touches his mood. Hanami dies, nothing. Jogo burns to death at Sukuna's feet. And this is the one moment the story lets Kenjaku feel something close to loss. But he doesn't regret the death or care about Jogo. He regrets the waste. He wanted to absorb and use the curse's power. It isn't, "I've lost a friend." It's, "I've lost a sample." And when Yuji beats Mahito down and moves to finish him, Kenjaku simply scoops Mahito up and thanks Yuji for it. He appreciates the boy raising the curse to full strength before the harvest. Yuji had done his gardening for him. He is enjoying the night. He just isn't in it the way everyone else is. Everyone else is bleeding. He is browsing. The easiest word to point at is sadist, but that one doesn't really land. The series has a true sadist standing right next to Kenjaku, making the difference impossible to miss. Sukuna relishes the malice. He calls the people in a burning city maggots and means it fondly, dragging out one fight just to make the losing last. When he hijacks Megumi's body and feels the boy's soul fighting back, he tracks down Tsumiki and murders her. He kills her to break Megumi completely. To Sukuna, a shattered enemy tastes better than a beaten one. For Sukuna, the suffering is the meal. Kenjaku never does this. In all his centuries, there is no scene of him savoring a death. No moment where the screaming is the point of it all. He will cause endless agony, but he doesn't linger in it. He doesn't twist the knife or stay to watch the light go out. To him, pain is not a pleasure. It is a byproduct. But something is unsettling about Kenjaku when viewed from this exact lens and contrasted with Sukuna. Neither man is really fueled by a grievance. But where Sukuna filled that emptiness with an appetite, craving the suffering, Kenjaku filled it with nothing at all. He is curious, not hungry. Sukuna is a monster. You can say that word and feel it work. Kenjaku has done as much or worse, but the word slides right off him. He isn't cruel and he isn't kind. There is no language for what remains when you strip both away, leaving only a mild interest in what happens next.

The strangest thing about Jujutsu's most dangerous schemer is that he almost never forces anyone's hand. A binding vow, the contract underwriting his entire existence, cannot be imposed. Both sides must know. Both sides must agree. The entire physics of Jujutsu runs on consent. And Kenjaku, who could physically break most of the cast, chooses to work within it. He offers. He does not seize. Sukuna took the offer, signing into the Culling Game because Kenjaku made it worth his while. Kashimo took it, too. An old warrior bored of a weak world, agreeing to become a cursed object just for one more legendary opponent. Kokichi Muta agreed to it too for his benefit. Nobody was dragged. Nobody was tricked. They were invited and they came. And even if it's not a binding vow, his alliance with the curses was just him feeding them lies and convincing them he is helping their cause. Yet to him, they are just breakable pieces on a board game. Even against people who would never agree to anything, he refuses to force them. He simply sets an object down where they will find it and lets human nature do the rest. A finger left in a detention center just to measure how strong Sukuna's leftovers had grown. He can go on without touching any soul and the bodies pile up anyway. He barely killed anyone on screen himself. A finger left in a boy's apartment, a curse trailing behind it, and a dead mother at the end. He never lifted a finger. He doesn't push people off the ledge. He builds the ledge and waits for them to lean. He doesn't even necessarily lie. He almost never speaks a falsehood. He just omits the context, which gives us the most absurd fact about him. A thousand-year-old sorcerer wearing a dead friend's face walked into the White House, asked the most powerful government on Earth to invade Japan, and walked out with a yes. No threats, no deception, no lies really. He told them the absolute truth. There are sorcerers, there is energy, there is power. Come and take it. Not like it takes much to convince the US to invade a country for some bullshit. Kenjaku merely left out the part where their soldiers would march into a death ritual to be fed to it one at a time. He didn't have to lie to a superpower. They volunteered. He did the same with the Chinese government and other big hitters, all funneled into Japan as fuel for his plan. So far, this has been almost light, just a man having a pleasant day. Where is the more disturbing-than-you-think part?

Let me show you a pleasant day at a temple. In the Meiji era, wearing the body of Noritoshi Kamo, Kenjaku crossed paths with a woman who had fled to a mountain temple. She carried something the world would hate her for: the ability to conceive children with curses. She sought sanctuary in a holy place. She found Kenjaku instead. He kept her. The record states he imprisoned her out of intellectual curiosity. Nine times he had her impregnated by a curse. Nine times he mixed his own blood into what grew inside her, manipulating the genetic outcome. Not even a guess, but the most obvious deduction would be that the ancient Noritoshi Kamo also had Blood Manipulation as the signature technique of the clan, and something that would allow Kenjaku to manipulate and mix his blood into the experiment. Nine times the pregnancy was cut short. The fetus harvested and kept in the space between alive and dead. Nine pregnancies. Nine abortions. Nine things sealed in the dark for 150 years. Nine brothers bound only by each other. Nine children engineered and unmade on purpose by a man who just wanted to see what would happen. And the woman? Nothing left to be said about her. That is the true horror. We don't even know her name, how she died, or if he even let her live long enough to understand what he had ripped out of her nine times. We're told exhaustion killed her. The records were destroyed, wiped clean like a chalkboard once the equation is solved. And it's definitely not out of shame. He has none of that. The math just didn't matter anymore. Intellectual curiosity. That is how history describes what happened at that temple. 150 years later, with a nation about to be poured into a meat grinder, the eldest of those nine asks Kenjaku, "Why? Why any of this?" And Kenjaku, bored of the question, offers those same two words: intellectual curiosity. The temple and the apocalypse, the woman and the world. The same small, pleasant reason underlies both. He just wanted to see how it would turn out.

It's just like a child with a crayon drawing, wondering what will turn out by the end of the sketch. Nine failed, yet he kept going because the tenth was coming and to him, number ten was everything. He just needed a specific father and a specific body, a specific cursed human pot. Itadori happened to be the son of the reincarnated soul of Sukuna's twin, the soul the King of Curses devoured in the womb over a thousand years ago. It had drifted loose through the centuries until it finally floated into Kenjaku's hands. The mother, Kaori, was already dead by that point. He just wore her corpse to the house like he does all the time. And then the entity that swaps bodies like winter coats got pregnant. Something he can outsource. Kenjaku carried the child for nine months in a stolen womb and gave birth. That is disturbing. The boy was built entirely to specification. A part of Sukuna sealed inside him at the moment of birth, making Yuji both the perfect cage for the King of Curses and a player already seated at a table before the game had even begun. He gave Yuji a heartbeat, but never a home. He cared about this experiment, but we need to be careful about that care. He didn't love Yuji. He looks at him the way a scientist looks at a petri dish where the reaction finally worked. To Kenjaku, care simply means investment. And even that investment had an expiration date because when Kenjaku dies, the merger passes to Sukuna, not to the son he birthed to hold it. Yuji was always the vessel, never the heir. Just a tool the work demanded, dropped the very second it was finished. We never really know the full intention behind him in the first place. Was it to make a vessel for Sukuna? Was it to make someone that surpasses Sukuna? Is Yuji his idea of the curse-infused man of tomorrow or just an experiment? There's also real irony because he built his son a perfect body from absolute nothing. Yet he doesn't have one of his own. If you strip away the stolen faces, the bodies, and the grand schemes, what really is left? A brain. A single gray organ that unstitches itself from one skull and sews itself into the next. That in itself is his technique. And the technique is an outward expression of an innate reality. Jujutsu sorcery is self-expression. And this is Kenjaku's sorcery. He isn't a grand spirit possessing the living. He is a piece of meat he can hold in both hands, crawling from corpse to corpse, wearing humanity until it rots. He has worn the face of a clan head, of a dead mother, of the man who was once Gojo's best friend. And every single one of them was left with that same stitched scar across the forehead. The seam, the evidence where he forced himself in. It is a scar he can easily heal but chooses not to. The stitching stays. The Frankensteinification does not get undone. It is the only honest mark he bears. It is what makes him him. It is what makes him Kenjaku. To us, you see Geto here. You see Kenjaku here. It is a literal seam admitting that a parasite lives inside. He is an unreadable, three-moves-ahead entity in the story. Yet underneath all that complexity lies the crudest thing imaginable. A brain in a borrowed skull that really knows how to smile.

Amidst all of this, there is only one person who looked at the grand performance and refused to be entertained by it. Tried to entertain it, but also stood against it. Yuki spent her entire life chasing Kenjaku's exact question. What is humanity supposed to become? But she reached the exact opposite conclusion. Kenjaku wanted to optimize, to drive humanity deeper into its own poison until it forced an evolution. Yuki wanted to cure, to cut cursed energy out at the absolute root. So not a single curse could ever be born again. He wanted humanity remade. She wanted it washed clean. That clash of ideals is what makes her his perfect counterweight. Yuki was a Star Plasma Vessel, a girl born with a barcode on her soul meant to be spent as fuel for any mortal. The sorcery system told her what her life was worth, and she simply said no. She just walked away from her assigned script. She spent her life trying to become the machine that hands out those tragic roles, fighting for the ungrateful millions who create the very monsters that kill her kind. Except she never really does that. She's not a pawn of Jujutsu. They have no hold, no control, no read on her. They don't even know her true power. But just like Gojo, she had nowhere else to go, to be the loner or to stay within the system. She didn't do it because it was a game. She did it because it was right and because someone had to. She was the only person in the entire tragedy who refused to treat sorrow like entertainment. And she was about to find out exactly who she was fighting. When Yuki finally stands before Tengen, one of the first questions that gets answered is, "Who is he? Who has been pulling these threats for a thousand years?" Tengen answers her plainly. The thing wearing Suguru Geto's face was once Noritoshi Kamo, and it calls itself Kenjaku. Yuki is old enough in the jujutsu craft to understand the ancient vocabulary the name is built from, and she reads it right back to Tengen. Then she lets the name go. It is a strange name for a monster, but she has to fight that monster first before she learns the true meaning of the name. This strategy was built entirely on a trap. Bait Kenjaku into opening his domain. Stall him with a simple domain and let Tengen dismantle his barrier from the outside before finishing him with Star Rage. It was a flawless calculation. But Kenjaku didn't play by the rules of modern sorcery. He opened an open-barrier domain, an insane feat only accomplished by Sukuna before him. A domain with no outer shell for Tengen to easily target. Yuki's simple domain was shredded to pieces in seconds. Struck by the full force of his guaranteed hit, her arm was crushed and the blood filled her mouth. Yet she did the unthinkable. She got back up, continued fighting, and she chose not to heal. Even Kenjaku urged her to heal. That was the one variable the mastermind had not calculated. He assumed she would pause to mend herself with reverse cursed technique before attacking again. But she didn't. And for one fleeting instant, the intellect that had perfectly read every human drive for a thousand years guessed wrong. Yuki did not do the rational thing. That's just what Yuki does. In the tradition of her son and his brother, she just punches the obstacle and stands her ground. But a single miscalculation didn't save her. Kenjaku kept the kill. Patient, he flooded her with curses to keep her occupied and fired a miniature Uzumaki straight through her torso before she could catch her breath. Before she continued to fight and die, she let Choso out, inspiring him to live better, inspiring him to forgive himself. Ironic how Kenjaku's own son found salvation through Kenjaku's foil and antithesis. And as she lay dying, he took her own past kindness and threw it back at her like a weapon. Just as Yuki offered the cursed human Choso the chance to live on as a human, Kenjaku handed her that sentiment back. With the blade turned around, "Die as a human," he told her. It was the wittiest thing he could think of to say to someone he was erasing. Even his cruelty just has a bit that landed well. In her final breath, she condensed her entire mass into a singularity, becoming a black hole to drag him into oblivion with her. But he survived. Of course, he did. He pulled the anti-gravity system that has been his clutch this whole fight, the innate technique of Kaori Itadori, Yuji's mother. Think about the poetry of that horror. He killed the one woman, the one mother, fighting for humanity's soul, using the hollowed-out technique of the mother he ruined the body of to create humanity's hero.

Yuki was entirely right about who he was, but she was wrong about his name. Yuki heard the net of compassion and the rope of salvation, saw a creature devoid of human empathy, and she found the name quite ironic. We let it slide, too. But it isn't just irony. Kenjaku, the name comes from Fuku Kenjaku Kannon, the Buddhist deity of ultimate mercy, who carries a snare woven from five colored strands. In ancient times, it was used to trap wild animals. Buddhism elevated it into an instrument of absolute salvation. The deity casts this net into the endless ocean of human suffering, catches those who are drowning, and pulls them to the far shore. The first two characters translate to "never empty." It is the rope that always comes back full. No one it touches is left behind. Now, if we examine that ancient vow along with his thousand-year history, Kenjaku cast a net. He never actively hunted anyone. A snare simply waits. He placed his rope in the water and let the drifting souls swim into it. Kashimo, dying of boredom in a world too weak to match him. Caught. Ancient sorcerers aching for one more taste. One more chance at life. One more chance at sorcery. All caught. Ancient and modern. Caught, too. A man struggling with the justice system he believes in. Caught. A struggling comedian trying to light his career. Caught. Modern superpowers hungry for an energy source to exploit. Caught. An entire nation of ordinary people who never realized they were drowning. Caught. Every single soul. And where was he pulling them? Well, to the other side, to the merger. Every soul in Japan pulled across the threshold of individuality and compressed into a singular entity. He was carrying all of humanity to the far shore, fulfilling the Bodhisattva's vow to the literal letter. Yuki was right that he possessed no compassion, but she was wrong to think it made his name a lie. A rope doesn't need to love you to pull you out of the water. He performs every mechanical motion of salvation with the soul of salvation completely scooped out of it. The name says never empty. The rope is always full. But the man holding the rope himself is entirely hollow. And that is why he could smile. A savior who genuinely believes in the far shore is the most desperate creature alive because everything rides on a successful arrival. But Kenjaku never needed a destination. If you remove the destination, salvation stops being a heavy burden. There is nothing to reach and therefore nothing to fail. There is only the rhythm of the rope, the pulling action. Out and back, out and back, full every single time for ten centuries. Just like the thread on his forehead and the bodies he swaps, the attempts at salvation, the attempts at pulling people, the casting of the net was never a means to an end to him. The casting itself was the fun.

This is how profoundly little the grand plan actually meant to him. Near the very end, right in the thick of their bizarre duel, Takaba pauses and asks the one question we've all been wanting to ask. Why? Why do any of this? Kenjaku answers by saying it is entertaining, but we can cut to the chase and call it intellectual curiosity. He is bored of this question and tries to move on, but Takaba is not finished. Takaba hits Kenjaku with the most devastating question anyone can ask him in the entire series. Would you throw all of this away if something more interesting and entertaining came along? Kenjaku doesn't hesitate. He says he might. Let's think about that. A thousand years of meticulous tailoring, experimentations, the nine Death Paintings, the tragedy at the Star Plasma Temple, the sealing of Gojo Satoru, an entire nation prepared as cattle for a cosmic ritual. And he would willingly walk away from all of that for a better punchline, for a better time. Because to him, the merger was never the destination itself. A goal is something you crave to reach, and craving is its own form of suffering. But Kenjaku doesn't suffer. The merger was simply the most complex toy on the table for him to play with. Set a stranger one down, and you could sweep the pieces onto the floor without a single backward glance. Kenjaku is this exact meme. He spent a millennium building a stage only to realize he preferred being part of the audience. There is no far shore. There is no ocean of salvation. There is only the next distraction to him. And that's why reaching it or even us seeing it does not really matter for his character. And that is quite the fracture in his armor. If the only thing that can move you is something more interesting, then the only thing that can stop you is the exact same thing. Not a more powerful sorcerer, not a superior intellectual genius, not a strategy, not an opposing philosophical take, not Yuki, sadly. Just something he has never seen before. Something like a broken comedian who refuses to stop trying to make him laugh.

Observe the stage they built together. Takaba's innate technique forced Kenjaku's final battle to manifest as a surreal comedy routine. And for the first time in ten centuries or more, the ancient mastermind was fully, helplessly delighted. He wasn't scheming here. He wasn't planning. He wasn't observing humanity or curses from behind protective glass. He wasn't experimenting. He was playing. He was playing along with someone else. For once, in his agonizingly long existence, he wasn't pulling the strings. He wasn't pulling threads and ropes. He was caught in the net himself, having the absolute time of his life. And that genuine joy is precisely what killed him. Let's circle back to the sketch, to the myth they were performing right at the end. Momotaro, the mythic hero who storms the demon's fortress with a surprise attack and cuts the warlord down. Kenjaku got so deeply lost playing the role of the demon king that he completely forgot he was in a war. He was in a fight. He never saw the real executioner coming. The one who knew Gojo. The one who defeated the body he stole. The one who operated outside of his strings. Okkotsu Yuta took his head clean off mid-joke. Kenjaku was too late on the anti-gravity technique because for once his mind wasn't calculating the next three to ten moves ahead. The sentence he was saying right before getting cut was that Takaba is hilarious, that he was having fun. And notice the way he exits the story. He does not rage. He doesn't beg for his life. He dies smiling, laughing, looking up at Takaba to tell him he was hilarious. The last full sentence when he was in a body is a genuine compliment of a failing comedian. Even as his head drops to the dirt, he doesn't panic. He simply states that his will would be inherited. The machine won't be stopped. And it is for a while because he engineered the Culling Game so perfectly that killing him changes absolutely nothing. Sukuna takes the plan off the ground like a dropped card and plays the next hand.

So, the narrative had one final opportunity to make this monster pay for a thousand years of sin, and it chose not to. Instead, he received the most beautiful death in the entire series, laughing on a stage, doing what he loved alongside someone who loved it just as much. It is said that sorcerers always die with regrets. Not this one. At the end of the story, chapters later, the story cuts to a quiet cafe where Takaba is bickering with a new comedy partner whose face remains deliberately obscured. He looks an awful lot like Suguru Geto. Maybe it is just Takaba's grief, bending reality to manifest the only man who ever truly got his jokes. Or maybe the net that never comes back empty finally found its way home. The story refuses to say. I find it interesting that we meet Kenjaku in a cafe. We meet him with allies he doesn't really see as equals, allies he doesn't see as allies, bonds to be manipulated. And at the end of the story, he or an image of him is in a cafe again. I'd like to imagine it's the same cafe. That's my own canon, with a true partner, with the one person that got him.

But go back to the beginning of it all. A thousand-year-old parasite on a stage trading bits, throwing jokes around, having the time of his life. It was the closest thing he ever came to something resembling a soul because the stage was the only place where his performance and his pleasure became the exact same thing. That entire sequence is one of the most beloved fights Gege Akutami ever drew, and it is one of the most genius. We laughed. The audience cheered when the show character finally got his moment in the sun for a few chapters while watching the literal architect of the apocalypse trade banter on a stage. We had a wonderful time, and so did he. That's the true horror of Kenjaku, really. It isn't that his mind is too alien for us to comprehend. It's that this one pure, unadulterated motive: I wonder what happens next. Wouldn't that be fun? He is the exact same curiosity that kept us tuning in for every chapter, for every release, for every leak, curious for every page, for every chapter. He just followed that urge a thousand years past the point where a normal human would have stopped. And just like everyone he caught in the net, just like everyone he pulled with a rope, he never invited us into his madness. He just welcomed us to the theater. And it's not just this theater. It's not just this fight. It's not just the Culling Game. It is the entirety of Jujutsu Kaisen. If you really think about it, it's the same road. You were walking it with him the entire time. You laughed, too.
